Reflection Cedric · after the full run

How the agents performed

On each ticket, I think the agents did a great job, because they followed my rules. I didn't want the Boss to be the one who just sends every ticket to everyone, and it didn't. The agents followed the flow. On ticket 101, the Boss sent the ticket to Accounting first, Accounting filed the invoice payment and passed it on to Inventory, and Inventory passed it to Customer Service to tell the customer. I appreciate how the agents worked together as a team. Once they finished their part, the work came back to the Boss. When it got back to the Boss, it was time for an approval. After a human (me) approved the payments, we ran the ticket again, the Boss saw that the payments had gone through, and the ticket was resolved.

What went well:
  • The Boss sent each ticket to the one agent who owned the problem first (Accounting, then Facilities, then Inventory), not to everyone.
  • The agents handed work to each other in a chain and then reported back up to the Boss.
  • Nothing was paid until a person approved it, and the Boss only closed tickets after it saw the payments go through.

Expected vs. actual

When I go back to the expected and actual sections, there were places on two tickets where what actually happened didn't line up cleanly with what I expected. Some of those differences meant extra work, and some just turned out differently than planned. As the agents work together more often, I believe they'll get smarter about each other.

Ideas for the priority map

  • Rank each payment by due date and type (rent, overdue vendor bills, then purchase orders), and show the balance left after each one, in order, on the Approvals panel.
  • Flag payments that aren't due yet (like rent 2 days early), and show what else that cash could fund. The person still decides.
  • Show money coming in, like a customer sale, with its expected date, so a discount order can be planned against when the cash will actually arrive.
  • When the full order doesn't fit, suggest the largest order that does. After both bills, $160 covers 7 hoodies ($154), so with the 8 in stock the club could get 15 of 20 now.

Ticket 103: the $104 gap, and how we could have made more money

On ticket 103 we needed $264 to fill the whole order (12 more hoodies × $22), but we only had $160 on hand after rent and invoice 501, which left us $104 short. The team did the safe thing: no purchase order, and the ticket stayed open. But safe isn't the same as profitable. We need to maximize profit, not just avoid overspending. At the $50 price, every hoodie earns $28 over its $22 cost, and every hoodie we didn't sell was $28 left on the table.

The agents were good at protecting cash, but nobody was in charge of finding the most profitable way to say yes. That's the job I'd give the Convener, along with asking for a deposit when a big order needs cash we don't have yet.

One new agent: a Convener

If I could add one agent with tools, it would be a Convener, someone who pulls everyone together so they work around the table together instead of passing a ticket down a flowchart. It would run as an ongoing conversation, a standing meeting, so the tickets aren't managed in silos. Every open ticket is on the table at once, so the team can decide what to prioritize.

How it differs from today: today, Boss works one ticket at a time and each ticket travels its own chain, so ticket 103 never "knew" that rent was competing for the same cash. A Convener would read the whole board first (all tickets, all bills, all stock), hear from each agent once, and then set the order before anyone acts.
